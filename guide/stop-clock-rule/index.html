The Stop Clock Rule: 60 Seconds Between Overs and the 5-Run Penalty

By KhelaBD editorial · Published Fri 6 Nov 2026 · Updated Tue 10 Nov 2026

The stop clock requires the fielding side to be ready to bowl the next over within 60 seconds of the previous one ending. The third umpire runs a clock shown on the big screen. The first two breaches in an innings (in Tests, in each block of 80 overs) bring a warning; every breach after that costs the fielding side five penalty runs, added to the batting side's total. It became a permanent playing condition in men's ODIs and T20Is in June 2024 and was extended to Test cricket from the 2025–27 World Test Championship cycle, where it helps teams reach the 90 overs scheduled in a day.

  • Warnings and the penalty
  • Which formats
  • Why it was introduced
  • What it is not

How it works

  1. When an over ends, the third umpire starts a 60-second clock, visible on the ground’s screen and on the broadcast. In Tests the clock counts up from zero to 60.
  2. The bowler must be ready to start the next over before the clock reaches zero.
  3. The on-field umpire can stop the clock for a fair delay: a batter is injured, a review is in progress, a new batter is coming in, drinks, or time lost by the batting side.

Warnings and the penalty

Breach in the inningsConsequence
FirstWarning
SecondFinal warning
Third and every one after5 penalty runs to the batting side

The warnings are per innings in limited-overs cricket. In Tests, the warnings reset after every 80 overs of an innings, lining up with the new ball.

Which formats

  • ODIs and T20Is: permanent since June 2024 after a trial that cut around 20 minutes from a T20I.
  • Tests: from the 2025–27 WTC cycle, first used in the Sri Lanka v Bangladesh Test at Galle in June 2025, alongside the existing over-rate fines and WTC points deductions for slow play.

Why it was introduced

Slow over rates had become routine: T20Is finishing 20 to 30 minutes late and Test days falling short of 90 overs. Fines on match fees did not change behaviour; a run penalty during the match does. Fans in Bangladesh notice it most in evening T20Is, which now finish closer to the scheduled time.

What it is not

The stop clock does not change the over-rate rules for captains’ fines or WTC point deductions; those still apply after the match. It also does not apply to the batting side. Batters are covered by a separate time-wasting rule added to the ICC playing conditions for international series starting on or after 1 October 2026: a batter who is not ready to face within the prescribed time gets a warning, then a final warning, and every later offence gives the fielding side five penalty runs.
